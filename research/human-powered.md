# Human powered journeys: 25 atlas candidates

Research shortlist, ranked for recognition, route evidence, and variety of walking, skiing, cycling, climbing, paddling, and rowing. “High” means a known trail, climbing line, or coastal itinerary; “Medium” means recoverable waypoints; “Low” means significant route uncertainty. These labels describe map geometry, not the legitimacy of the achievement. Even high-confidence historical routes are not GPS traces.

## Editorial and mapping cautions

- Label each segment by mode. In around-the-world cycling journeys, ship or air transfers are gaps rather than bicycle track. Some ocean crossings in human-powered circumnavigations used rowing or pedal boats, which should be shown as such.
- Coastlines, rivers, and mountaineering routes can guide a reconstruction, but they are not the actual historical track. Ocean rows in particular need voyage logs or GPS before drawing anything more precise than a broad corridor through waypoints.
- Respect source qualifications: the Appalachian Trail Conservancy describes Shaffer’s achievement as the first *reported* thru-hike; Guinness notes that Outen was towed near Adak and Aston received resupply.
- The Appalachian Trail’s Georgia terminus was Mount Oglethorpe for both Shaffer in 1948 and Gatewood in 1955, not Springer Mountain. The [Smithsonian’s Shaffer exhibit](https://amhistory.si.edu/documentsgallery/exhibitions/appalachian_trail_2.html) and a [contemporary 1955 ATC report](https://appalachiantrail.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Trail_Years.pdf) identify their starts; [ATC dates the relocation to 1958](https://journeys.appalachiantrail.org/issue/fall-2025/the-next-100-years/). The summit coordinate is an approximate modern geographic reference, not a recovered 1948 or 1955 trail survey.
- For polar and high-altitude travel, a world slippy map can present the journey’s geography, but altitude and steepness need an inset or elevation profile. Do not interpolate a mountain route across impassable terrain.
