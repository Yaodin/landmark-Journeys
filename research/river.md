# River journeys: 25 atlas candidates

Rank is editorial. A journey belongs here when its central mapped leg follows a named river; ocean approaches and overland transfers should be separate segments. **Geometry confidence**: H = contemporary chart/log or well documented river leg; M = documented river and endpoints with uncertain historic channel or transfers; L = important gaps or disputed course. Modern river centerlines are **not** necessarily historical channels: dams, cutoffs, distributaries, and shifting mouths matter. Many journeys below involved Indigenous waterways and expertise long before European archival records; do not call them a river's "discovery."

**Implementation notes:** Preserve separate river, portage, overland, and sea segments. In particular, the Mekong mission was thousands of kilometres overland; Lewis and Clark crossed a watershed on foot; Franklin returned overland. Route snapping to present river geometry requires reviewing major engineering changes on the Colorado, Mississippi, Columbia, Fraser, and Nile. The historical route may follow a former channel or a dry former delta. Use uncertainty bands for the Amazon and Congo expeditions where source waypoints are sparse.

Powell endpoint correction: [NPS Lake Mead history](https://www.nps.gov/lake/learn/historyculture/early-explorers-and-settlers.htm) and the [USGS 1869 route account](https://pubs.usgs.gov/unnumbered/70039264/report.pdf) place the boat-journey end at the historic Virgin–Colorado confluence on 30 August, **not** at the St. Thomas town point previously used. The confluence is now under Lake Mead; its plotted coordinate is a regional estimate near historic Rioville, not a surveyed 1869 position. [NPS history](https://www.nps.gov/media/video/view.htm?id=410B30E2-CB92-40FC-BF4D-4267D86612DC) documents the crew separation at Separation Canyon, also added as a regional waypoint. Neither change recovers the pre-dam channel.
